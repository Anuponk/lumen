How you learn in Lumen

  1. Several quests make up each constellation. Every solved puzzle lights up one of its stars in its real position.
  2. The shape appears gradually. When two connected stars are lit, the line between them lights up as well.
  3. My Sky keeps your constellations together. Return any time to see their shapes and names.

Star positions come from the Hipparcos catalogue, and the figures follow the Western tradition documented by Stellarium. Read the How to play guide for the puzzle rules.

Constellations in the first Adventure

The free first Adventure includes 100 quests across 10 constellations. Meet Ursa Major (Big Dipper), Cassiopeia, Crux (Southern Cross), Lyra, Cygnus (Northern Cross), Leo, Scorpius, Gemini, Taurus and Aquila. Later Adventures take you through all 88 official constellations.

Find them in the night sky

These pointers are for observers in mainland France. Find a dark spot away from city lights.

  • Ursa Major and Cassiopeia are visible to the north year-round. Follow the line through the two stars at the outer edge of the Big Dipper’s bowl. Continue about five times the distance between them to reach Polaris. W-shaped Cassiopeia lies on the other side of Polaris.
  • Lyra, Cygnus and Aquila. Their brightest stars, Vega, Deneb and Altair, form the Summer Triangle high in the sky in summer.
  • Scorpius sits low on the southern horizon in summer. Look for its reddish star Antares.
  • Leo is visible in spring. Look for Regulus and the constellation’s backward-sickle shape.
  • Taurus and Gemini are close to Orion in winter. Taurus includes orange Aldebaran and the Pleiades star cluster. Look for Castor and Pollux in Gemini.
  • The Southern Cross is visible from the Southern Hemisphere and lies below the horizon from mainland France. Use it to find the south celestial pole.

Lumen teaches the shapes. A star chart or a planetarium app such as Stellarium can help you find out what is visible tonight.
